The format

How BP works

British Parliamentary is the world's most widely used university debating format, and the one used at the World Universities Debating Championship. Here's everything you need for your first debate.

The basics Teams & order Speaker roles POIs Prep time Judging Motions Glossary
01

The basics

Every BP debate has four teams of two speakers. Two teams form the Government (or Proposition), which supports the motion. The other two form the Opposition, which argues against it.

The twist is that you're not just competing against the other side. All four teams are ranked 1st to 4th, so you also need to beat the team sitting next to you on your own bench. Closing teams can't simply repeat what the opening teams said. They have to bring something new.

03

What each speaker does

Every speech lasts 7 minutes (some beginner and university-internal events use 5). Here's what each position is expected to do.

1 · Opening Government

Prime Minister

  • Defines the motion and sets out the Government's model or proposal
  • Presents the team's first arguments
2 · Opening Opposition

Leader of the Opposition

  • Rebuts the Prime Minister's case
  • Presents the Opposition's first arguments, which may include a counter-proposal
3 · Opening Government

Deputy Prime Minister

  • Rebuts the Leader of the Opposition and rebuilds their own case
  • Adds further arguments or develops the PM's arguments in more depth
4 · Opening Opposition

Deputy Leader of the Opposition

  • Continues rebuttal of the Government
  • Adds further Opposition arguments or develops the existing ones
5 · Closing Government

Member of Government

  • Brings the extension: new material that moves the debate forward and sets CG apart from OG
  • Must stay consistent with the opening team (no "knifing")
6 · Closing Opposition

Member of the Opposition

  • Rebuts the Government bench, including CG's extension
  • Brings CO's own extension, distinct from Opening Opposition
7 · Closing Government

Government Whip

  • Summarises the debate from the Government's point of view, usually organised around the key clashes
  • Rebuts the Opposition and shows why their extension matters most
8 · Closing Opposition

Opposition Whip

  • Gives the last speech of the debate, summarising it for the Opposition
  • Answers the Government and shows why CO's contribution wins. By convention, no new arguments
04

Points of Information

During an opponent's speech you can stand up and offer a Point of Information (POI), a short question or statement of up to about 15 seconds. The speaker chooses whether to accept it ("Yes, please") or decline it ("No, thank you").

  • The first and last minute of each speech are protected, so no POIs are allowed then. Judges usually signal the start and end of the open period.
  • POIs can only be offered to the other side, not to the team on your own bench.
  • Speakers are expected to accept one or two POIs in a speech. Taking none can count against you.
  • Offering good POIs, especially as a closing team, is a great way to stay visible in the debate.
05

Preparation time

The motion is announced, sometimes with an info slide giving background, and teams get 15 minutes to prepare with their partner. Teams are assigned their positions by draw, so you don't pick your side.

No internet. At most tournaments you can't use phones, laptops or other electronic devices during prep. You prepare from what you already know, with pen and paper. Exact rules on printed material vary by tournament.

Debaters are also expected to follow general world events, because motions can cover anything from economics and international relations to philosophy, technology, culture and sport.

06

How judging works

Debates are judged by one adjudicator or a panel, led by a chair. After the debate the panel discusses and agrees on a ranking of the four teams, then gives an oral adjudication explaining the decision and offering feedback.

  • Judges assess the debate as an informed, average, reasonable person would. They don't use their own specialist knowledge or personal opinions.
  • What matters is how persuasive your contribution was: well-explained, relevant arguments that engage with the other teams.
  • Style counts only insofar as it makes you clearer and more persuasive. There's no prize for accents or big words.
  • Each speaker also gets individual speaker points, typically on a 50–100 scale, used for speaker rankings and tie-breaks.
07

Types of motion

Motions start with "This House…" (TH). Common forms include:

This House would…

A policy: Government proposes doing something. THW ban private cars from city centres.

This House believes that…

A claim about what is true or valuable. THBT democracy is best protected by courts.

This House regrets…

Is the world worse because something happened or exists? THR the rise of influencer culture.

This House, as X, would…

Argue from a specific actor's perspective. TH, as the EU, would…

Example motions above are illustrative.

08

Glossary

The vocabulary you'll hear at your first session.

Motion
The topic being debated, announced before prep.
Info slide
Background information shown with the motion to clarify a term or context.
Model
The specific proposal set out by the Prime Minister on a policy motion.
Extension
The new contribution a closing team brings to the debate.
Clash
A key point of disagreement between the two sides.
Rebuttal
Responding to and taking apart the other side's arguments.
POI
Point of Information: a short interjection offered during an opponent's speech.
Knifing
Contradicting your own bench. A serious mistake.
Squirrelling
Defining the motion unreasonably to dodge the intended debate.
Adjudicator / Adj
A judge. The chair leads the panel. Wings assist and vote.
IV
An "inter-varsity": a tournament between universities.
Tab & Break
The tab is the tournament ranking. "Breaking" means reaching the elimination rounds.
Swing team
A team made up on the day to fill an empty slot in the draw.
Iron-person
One speaker giving both of their team's speeches when a partner is missing.
